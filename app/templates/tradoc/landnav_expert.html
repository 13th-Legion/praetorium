Navigating Different Terrains

Navigating accurately in featureless terrain takes mental concentration, patience, advanced navigation skills, practice, and experience. The techniques already learned will help you navigate anywhere — but special environments require special considerations.

Dead Reckoning

Dead reckoning combines azimuth (direction) data with pacing (distance) data. The "dead" is derived from "ded." — an abbreviation of "deduced." It is navigation by logical deduction.

  • Not as accurate as other forms, but sufficient when it's the only resource available
  • It is an estimated position — you are not at the exact spot, just somewhere near it
  • For short distances (a few miles), cumulative errors are acceptable

Three Fundamental Steps

  1. Begin from a positive fix — a point you are certain of and can locate on the map
  2. Maintain accurate data of distance and direction for each leg of your route
  3. Use this data to update your position on the map

Plot your estimated position: every hour on the hour (30 minutes is more accurate), after every change of direction, and after every rest stop.

Desert Navigation

There are three types of deserts: Mountain, Rocky Plateau, and Sand Dune.

Sand dune desert — limited visual cues

Rocky plateau with hidden arroyos

Sand Dune Deserts

  • With few visual cues or during sandstorms, proceed by dead reckoning
  • For short distances, use your footsteps behind you as a back azimuth reference
  • Distances are deceptive! A landmark that seems near may be 20-30 miles distant
  • Rule of thumb: multiply your most conservative distance/time estimate by a factor of three
  • Animals tend to stay near water sources (oases)

Rocky Plateau Deserts

  • Hidden canyons and deep washes (arroyos) can impede progress — you may hike 10-20 miles out of the way to work around an impassable canyon
  • Study your map very carefully with your current surroundings
  • Use dead reckoning, very close intermediate landmarks, baselines, and back azimuths

Mountain Deserts

Mountain desert — use aspect of slope to determine your position

  • Use Aspect of Slope — take a compass reading along an imaginary line straight down the slope (cutting contour lines at ~90°)
  • Check the map: knowing the direction of slope helps track your location even in poor visibility
  • An altimeter (if available) can pinpoint your exact position along the bearing

Jungle Navigation

Dense jungle — rely heavily on dead reckoning and available trails

  • Dense vegetation forces use of wildlife paths, ridge lines, streams, dry river beds, or established trails
  • Rely heavily on dead reckoning — navigation is complicated by inability to make straight-line movements
  • Be prepared to follow a meandering course that takes twice as long as the same distance in clear terrain
  • Travel during the day; get off established paths at night (predators use them)
  • A lost navigator can eventually find civilization by following any body of water with a downstream flow
  • Terrain analysis, constant compass use, and accurate pace count are essential

Snow / Arctic Navigation

Arctic/snow terrain — featureless landscape reduces depth perception

  • Near the magnetic pole, always question compass accuracy — back up with celestial observation, shadow, or watch technique
  • Snow changes the landscape: depressions level out, drifts fill ravines, trails disappear, bodies of water look like open fields
  • An ALL WHITE environment reduces depth perception and judgment of distance
  • Valley floors are often easiest to navigate in winter — sheltered from wind, underbrush covered by snow
  • In good weather, dead reckoning works well on glaciers and open snowfields

Fog / Low Visibility

Fog can reduce visibility suddenly — always know your position

  • Know where you are at all times — visibility can deteriorate suddenly in any environment
  • If you see fog approaching, immediately fix your map position
  • Rule #1: Always trust your compass
  • Use dead reckoning, visible intermediate landmarks, human reference points, back azimuths, thumb the map, baselines

Snow Whiteout

  • Danger of vertigo — no clear difference between sky and ground
  • Toss a snowball ahead: if it sticks in mid-air → upward slope; if it lands lower → downward slope; if it disappears → back off quickly (steep drop or cliff)

Night Navigation

Darkness presents its own challenges, but the techniques and principles are the same as day navigation. Success depends on rehearsals during the planning phase — detailed map analysis, predetermined azimuths and distances.

Dark Adaptation

  • Allow your eyes 30 minutes in the dark without bright lights (5 minutes minimum)
  • Exposure to bright lights (matches, flashlights, vehicle headlights) can take up to 45 minutes to recover from
  • Wear sunglasses during the day to protect night vision — dark glasses with neutral gray tint recommended
  • Red-lighted method: Spend 20 minutes in a red-lighted area + 10 minutes in the dark — saves time by allowing equipment checks first
  • If in a white-lighted area, wear red goggles for 30 minutes before heading out

Red goggles preserve night vision during preparation

Protecting Night Vision

  • Any bright light can temporarily ruin night vision
  • Use a flashlight with a red lens filter — it won't damage night adaptation
  • If you must look into a lighted area, cover one eye to retain night vision in the other
  • Use red light sticks for map reading at night
  • Night vision goggles (NVG) impede dark adaptation, but if you adapt first, full adaptation returns in ~2 minutes after removing them

Scanning the Terrain

  • Practice seeing things without looking directly at them
  • Scan slowly from right to left (or left to right)
  • Off-Center Vision: Look 10° above, below, or to either side of an object — peripheral vision maintains contact
  • Bleach-Out Effect: An object viewed longer than 2-3 seconds tends to disappear. Shift your eyes between off-center points to keep picking it up

Night navigation requires heightened awareness and careful movement

Traveling at Night

  • The basic technique is dead reckoning
  • Move slowly — slide your feet, using toes to sense obstacles
  • You tend to take shorter steps at night — adjust your pace count
  • Check your map position every 30 minutes
  • Lift knees higher than normal to reduce stumbling over rocks, roots, and low branches
  • Stay off paths that wild predators use for hunting

Night Compass Technique

Luminous markings visible in darkness

Tritium compass glowing at night

Special compass features for night use: luminous markings, bezel ring clicks (3° per click), and luminous sighting dots.

  1. Step 1: Plot your course on the map (e.g., 327° azimuth)
  2. Step 2: Align the North Arrow and Luminous Bezel Line with the Black Index Line. Calculate: 360° − 327° = 33°. Divide by 3 = 11 clicks. Rotate bezel 11 clicks right
  3. Step 3: Using Center-Hold method, rotate your body until the North Arrow and Luminous Bezel Line align
  4. Step 4: Keep them aligned and proceed forward in the direction of the luminous sighting dots

Use the luminous sighting dots as a visual cue for aligning your body with the compass during night navigation.

Sustainment

Land navigation is a highly perishable skill. The best way to maintain it is to get "dirt time" — get out there with a map and compass. The navigator must continually use acquired skills to remain proficient.

Sustainment Program

Use the building-block approach — theory, practice, testing, skill-building. All navigators should receive sustainment training at least twice a year.

  • Map reading instruction/review
  • Land navigation skills training
  • Dead reckoning training/practice
  • Terrain association training/practice
  • Written and field examinations

Develop individual Training Modules usable for group programs or individual self-study.

Field briefing using a terrain model — sustainment training in action

Train-the-Trainer Program

  • Appoint a cadre of instructors as primary and alternate for land navigation training
  • Each instructor practices their module until fully prepared
  • Instructors train peer instructors, who then train subordinate members
  • Use training modules; have students go through each until they can demonstrate expertise
  • Conduct training at the lowest level possible

Setting Up a Land Navigation Course

Topographic map used for plotting a land navigation training course

Standards (Recommended)

  • A course is made up of 3-5 lanes (routes)
  • Total distance per lane: 2,700m - 11,000m
  • Position stakes (legs) per lane: 7-9
  • Distance between stakes: 300m - 1,200m
  • Time allowed: 3-4 hours
  • Training area: at least 25 square kilometers (5km × 5km)

Setup Sequence

  1. Plot position stake locations on the map
  2. Fabricate position stakes (consider visibility requirements)
  3. Survey the stake locations
  4. Certify the course — have subject matter experts (SMEs) negotiate each lane
  5. Prepare course requirement sheets
  6. Complete a risk assessment of the training area
  7. Begin teaching

Course Types

Three example formats:

  • Type 1 — Stake Number: Navigator receives azimuths/distances and records the stake number found at each point
  • Type 2 — Azimuth/Distance: Navigator receives azimuths/distances and must record stake numbers — errors reveal where navigation went wrong
  • Type 3 — UTM Coordinates: Navigator receives only grid coordinates and must navigate using map and protractor skills

Each navigator's answer sheet should record: start time, azimuth/distance/stake for each leg, stop time, and elapsed time.
