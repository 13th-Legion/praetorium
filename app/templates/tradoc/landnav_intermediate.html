Making Sense of Direction

The Problem of Lateral Drift

Lateral drift: even small errors compound over distance, causing you to miss the destination

A hiker's path is never perfectly straight — small detours around logs, boulders, and water create lateral drift. The compass provides only the forward component of navigation; by itself it cannot prevent lateral errors. The key is to be conscious of detours, keep them short, and try to zig as often as you zag.

Setting a Course — Three Methods

Method 1 — Direct Alignment

  1. Rotate the compass until the desired azimuth (e.g., 120°) aligns with the Black Index Line
  2. Rotate the bezel until the Luminous Bezel Line aligns with the North Arrow
  3. Follow the set azimuth, keeping the North Arrow aligned with the Bezel Line

Method 2 — Subtraction

  1. Align the North Arrow and Luminous Bezel Line with the Black Index Line
  2. Subtract desired azimuth from 360° (e.g., 360° − 120° = 240°)
  3. Rotate bezel until Luminous Bezel Line aligns with 240°
  4. Rotate compass until Bezel Line aligns with North Arrow — Index Line will show 120°

Method 3 — Click Count

  1. Align Luminous Bezel Line with the Black Index Line
  2. Divide desired azimuth by 3 to get clicks (e.g., 120° ÷ 3 = 40 clicks left). For azimuths above 180°: (360° − azimuth) ÷ 3 = clicks right
  3. Rotate bezel the calculated number of clicks
  4. Rotate compass until Bezel Line aligns with North Arrow

Using Intermediate Landmarks

To combat lateral drift, use intermediate landmarks — trees, boulders, hilltops, or any visible feature along your bearing. Sight a landmark on your azimuth, walk to it, then pick the next one. Continue until you reach your destination. Keep distances between landmarks short for better accuracy.

Best practices:

  • Make careful, accurate sightings on both destination and intermediate landmarks
  • Recheck bearings often to avoid accumulation of small errors
  • Use bearings over short distances when possible
  • Continually relate your progress to the map
  • Aim for a line (like a stream) rather than a point (like a waterfall on that stream)
  • Line up two distant objects on your bearing that will always be in sight — when you detour, correct by moving until they realign

Navigating Around Obstacles

Visible Landmark Method

If you can see a landmark directly on your bearing on the other side of an obstacle (e.g., a lake), walk the obstacle's perimeter until you reach that landmark, then continue on your bearing.

90° Box Method

  1. Turn 90° left and pace count until you clear the obstacle
  2. Turn 90° right and walk until you clear the obstacle again
  3. Turn 90° right and pace count the same number of paces as step 1
  4. Turn 90° left — you are back on your original bearing

Back Azimuths

A back azimuth is the reverse direction of an azimuth — comparable to an "about face."

  • If the azimuth is less than 180°: ADD 180°
  • If the azimuth is more than 180°: SUBTRACT 180°

Example: Forward azimuth 15° → back azimuth = 15° + 180° = 195°. Forward azimuth 277° → back azimuth = 277° − 180° = 97°.

Tracking Present Location

Make it a habit to keep your map and compass handy and refer to them every hour or so to locate your position (more often in low visibility). To find where you are, relate your position to features you can see and identify on the map.

Resection (Map Only)

Resection: determining your position by taking bearings to two known landmarks

Prerequisite: At least two (preferably three) identifiable points on the landscape that also appear on the map.

  1. Orient the map with the landscape
  2. Using a straight edge, lay flat on the map and align with the first landmark, then draw a line
  3. Repeat for the second landmark
  4. Where the lines intersect — you are there

Drawing resection lines on a map to find your position

Modified Resection (Map or Compass)

Prerequisite: You must be on a linear feature (trail, road, river, ridge line).

  1. Orient the map with the landscape
  2. Align a straight edge with an identifiable landmark and draw a line (or take a compass bearing)
  3. Where the line crosses your linear feature — that is your position

Intersection (Two Compass Bearings)

Prerequisite: Two identifiable points on the landscape that also appear on the map.

  1. Orient the map to Magnetic North (lay compass on MN line, rotate map until compass reads 0°, secure with rocks)
  2. Take a compass bearing to the first landmark
  3. Lay the compass on the map at the first landmark, rotate until the bearing aligns with the index line, draw a line
  4. Repeat for the second landmark
  5. Where the lines cross — that is approximately your position

Triangulation (Three Compass Bearings)

Same procedure as intersection, but with three landmarks. The three lines form a small triangle — your position is within it. The more accurate your bearings, the smaller the triangle.

MN Lines Alternate Technique

Magnetic North lines drawn on the map for faster bearing work

Instead of converting between Grid North and Magnetic North for every bearing, draw Magnetic North lines directly on your map before going into the field:

  1. Update the declination if using an older map (add or subtract the annual change)
  2. Draw parallel MN lines across the map, accurately aligned with the map's MN line
  3. When taking bearings, set the bezel to the landmark azimuth, then lay the compass on the map with the luminous bezel line parallel to the MN lines
  4. Draw a line from the landmark through — no G-M angle conversion needed

UTM Grid Coordinates

UTM grid system on a topographic map

The Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) system divides the world into 60 zones at 6° intervals. Grid lines are always 1 kilometer apart, making distance estimation easy.

  • Easting (vertical lines) is always read first, then Northing (horizontal lines) — "Read RIGHT, then UP"
  • Increasing numbers mean you are traveling north or east; decreasing means south or west
  • The more digits, the more precise: 4 digits = 1,000m area; 6 digits = 100m area; 8 digits = 10m area; 10 digits = 1m area

Use a coordinate scale protractor aligned within the grid square to determine precise coordinates. This system is excellent for communicating positions via radio.

Determining Distance

Determining travel distance is the most common error encountered while moving. It is essential to learn methods for accurately measuring, pacing, or estimating distances both on the map and on the ground.

Map Distance Measurement

Measuring straight-line distance using a paper edge and bar scale

Straight Line Distance

  1. Lay a straight-edged piece of paper so it touches both points, extending past them
  2. Make a tick mark at each point on the paper edge
  3. Move the paper to the graphic bar scale, align the right tick with a printed number so the left tick falls in the extension scale
  4. Read the distance

Measuring curvature distance along a road or trail

Curvature Distance

For curved features (trails, rivers), use one of these methods:

  • Straight segments: Place a tick mark at the start. Align the paper edge along each straight portion, marking where the curve departs. Repeat. Measure total from first to last tick on the bar scale.
  • String method: Lay a string along the curved route, then straighten and measure against the bar scale.
  • Map wheel: Roll the wheel along the route; it records distance in centimeters or inches for bar scale conversion.

Slope Distance

Horizontal map distance doesn't account for slope. To calculate actual travel distance including elevation change:

  • Slope % = (Vertical Distance ÷ Horizontal Distance) × 100
  • Slope degrees = (Vertical Distance ÷ Horizontal Distance) × 57.3
  • Add the slope percentage to horizontal distance for total: HD + (Slope% × HD) = Total Distance

Slopes above 12% are a concern for hikers. Slopes above 25% mean you are climbing.

Distance by Speed

Estimate hiking time using the U.S. Army method:

Time = (Distance ÷ Speed) + (1 hour per 1,000 feet of elevation gain)

Example: 4 miles at 4 MPH with 2,000 ft gain = 1 hr + 2 hrs = 3 hours.

Distance by Pace Count

Ranger Pacing Beads — pull one bead per 100 meters walked

Pace counting is essential for off-trail navigation, dead reckoning, and any situation where a map and compass alone aren't enough. One pace = every time your left foot touches the ground (every 2 steps).

Establishing Your Pace Count

  1. Measure exactly 100 meters on three types of ground: flat/easy terrain, rougher terrain with some slope, and steep hill terrain
  2. Walk each course counting paces (with pack and boots if that's your field setup)
  3. Record your pace count for each terrain type
  4. Memorize all three values

Use Ranger Pacing Beads to track distance — pull one bead per 100 meters walked. Create a personal pace table for quick reference.

The hardest thing to develop is a feel for adjusting your pace count when weaving through trees, shrubs, and boulders.

Distance by Estimation

Visual distance estimation technique

100-Meter Unit-of-Measure Method

Visualize 100-meter increments on the ground. For ranges up to 500 meters, count the increments between you and the object. Beyond 500 meters, find the halfway point, count increments to it, and double.

Factors affecting estimation: Objects appear closer when brightly lit, in clear air, across water/flat terrain, or when looking downhill. Objects appear farther in poor light, fog, narrow fields of view, or when looking uphill.

Visualizing 100-meter increments on the ground

Rule-of-Thumb Method

Your arm is approximately 10× longer than the distance between your eyes. Use this ratio:

  1. Hold one arm straight out, thumb up
  2. Close one eye, align one edge of your thumb with one edge of the target
  3. Without moving, switch eyes — your thumb appears to "jump"
  4. Estimate how far it jumped in units of the target's known width
  5. Multiply: (target width) × (number of jumps) × 10 = distance

Example: Barn estimated at 20m wide, thumb jumps 4 barn-widths → 20 × 4 × 10 = 800m.

Rule-of-thumb method: using your thumb and eye separation to estimate distance

Time Method

Keep track of starting time, rest breaks, and hiking pace. A simple formula:

  • Allow 1.2 minutes per 100 meters horizontal distance (at 5 km/h)
  • Add 1 minute per 10 meters of ascent
  • For slower groups (4 km/h): use 1.5 minutes per 100 meters
  • On gentle descents, ignore height loss; on steep descents, allow ~1 minute per 30 meters

Quick mental math: use the 12× table and move the decimal. 300m → 3 × 12 = 36 → 3.6 minutes.
