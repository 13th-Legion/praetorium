The Lensatic Compass

Description

The genuine lensatic compass differs from the traditional "orienteering" compass. Preferred by the military for its precision and durability, it is designed to take hyper-accurate bearings for land navigation and directing artillery fire.

  • Battle tested — shock, water, and sand proof; functional from −50°F to +150°F
  • Uses a retractable lens to read the bearing while simultaneously sighting an object
  • Luminous markings allow navigation in low-light and night conditions
  • Copper induction dampening system slows magnet rotation without liquids
  • Employs a "card" type floating dial for single-handed operation
  • Deep-well design allows global use with minimal accuracy loss from dial tilt
  • Retractable lens locks the dial jewel bearing when stowed to reduce wear

The genuine military compass is made by Cammenga. Cheap imitations break easily, lack accuracy, and display false features.

Parts and Features

Cover

  • Sighting Wire — front sight used with rear sight for sighting landmarks for azimuth headings
  • Luminous Sighting Dots — used in low-light and night navigation; visual cue for body alignment
  • Graduated Straight Edge — upper half of a standard 1:50,000 scale map ruler for measuring distances. Each tick mark represents 100 meters on a 1:50,000 map. Every 5 ticks = 1 cm; approximately every 13 ticks = 1 inch

Base

  • Bezel Ring — clicks when turned; 120 clicks for full 360° rotation (each click = 3°)
  • Luminous Bezel Line — marks course direction during day or night navigation
  • Floating Dial — black scale (mils), red scale (degrees), set in a deep tub for global use
  • Luminous Magnetic Arrow — always points to magnetic north
  • Fixed Index Line — read azimuth heading here
  • Thumb Loop — holds compass with the thumb

Close-up of the floating dial with cardinal directions

  • Lanyard Ring — for attaching string or rope

Lens/Rear Sight

  • Used to read the dial and as a rear sight for sighting objects
  • Also serves as a locking device — locks the dial jewel bearing when closed
  • Must be opened more than 45° to allow dial to float freely
  • When traveling, fold rear sight completely down to lock the dial and prevent vibration damage

Floating Dial Scales

Floating dial showing both mils (black) and degrees (red) scales

Mils (Black Scale)

Used mainly in artillery, tank, and mortar gunnery, and for very accurate azimuth land navigation.

  • 6,400 mils to a circle (U.S. military standard; derived from 2π × 1000 ≈ 6,283 milliradians, rounded to 6,400)
  • Small marks = 20 mils; Big marks = 100 mils; Numbers = 200 mils
  • N = 6400(0), E = 1600, S = 3200, W = 4800
  • 17.78 mils = 1 degree; 8.89 mils = ½ degree

Degrees (Red Scale)

  • 360 degrees to a circle
  • Red marks = 5°; Big marks = 10°; Numbers = 20°
  • N = 0°, E = 90°, S = 180°, W = 270°

Handling and Inspection

When obtaining a new compass, check that:

  • The dial does not stick
  • Sighting wire is not bent
  • Glass and other parts are not broken
  • Numbers on the dial are legible
  • Bezel ring makes distinct clicks when rotated
  • Index line on the cover glass bisects the sight slot

Discard any compass with more than ±3° variation. The lensatic compass is accurate to ½° (better with the mils scale).

Effects of Metal and Electricity

These sources affect compass performance during use. Maintain minimum distances:

  • 180 ft / 55 m — High tension power lines
  • 33 ft / 10 m — Trucks, cars, barbed wire
  • 6 ft / 2 m — Hunting rifle
  • 1 ft / ½ m — Knife, flashlight, binoculars, camera
  • Inches — Belt buckle, paper clip, jewelry

Calibration

Checking compass calibration on a known line of direction

A compass in good working condition is very accurate, but should be checked periodically on a known line of direction.

  1. Note the calibration point azimuth (the known magnetic azimuth from a calibration point to a designated point)
  2. Shoot an azimuth from the calibration point using the compass-to-cheek technique
  3. Ensure you check for effects from eyeglasses, watches, rings — wear the same items you'll use in the field
  4. Compare azimuths. If your compass reads higher than the known azimuth, add the difference to future readings; if lower, subtract it
  5. Write the ±error on the back of the compass for reference

Sighting Techniques

Compass-to-cheek sighting technique

Compass-to-Cheek Method (Primary)

The most accurate technique for taking azimuth bearings. Used almost exclusively for sighting.

  1. Open the cover to about 60° so the sighting wire is visible through the lens
  2. Raise the lens to about 45° so you can read the dial through it
  3. Hold the compass up to your cheek, looking through the rear sight slot
  4. Align the sighting wire with the target
  5. Read the azimuth through the lens at the fixed index line

Center-Hold Method

Center-hold: compass dial view

Center-hold: proper two-hand grip

Less precise but faster; usable under all visibility conditions.

  1. Open the cover to form a straight edge with the base
  2. Pull the rear sight to the rearmost position to allow the dial to float freely
  3. Place your thumb through the thumb loop, form a steady base with your third and fourth fingers, extend your index finger along the side
  4. With the other hand, place your thumb between the rear sight and bezel ring, extend the index finger along the other side, wrap remaining fingers
  5. Pull your elbows firmly into your sides — the compass should be between chin and belt
  6. Turn your entire body toward the object, pointing the compass cover directly at it
  7. Look down and read the azimuth from the fixed black index line

Following an Azimuth (Center-Hold)

  1. Turn your body until the desired azimuth aligns with the black index line
  2. Without turning the compass, rotate the bezel ring until the luminous bezel line aligns with the north arrow
  3. Once set, leave the bezel in place
  4. Keep the north arrow aligned with the luminous bezel line and proceed forward

The Topographic Map

A map is a graphic representation of the earth's surface drawn to scale, as seen from above. Understanding terrain features is essential for reading what the map depicts. It uses colors, lines, symbols, and labels to represent features found on the ground. The finest maps are worthless unless the map user knows how to read them.

Map Colors

Five basic colors are used on topographic maps:

  • Brown — Contour lines (relief and elevation)
  • Black — Man-made features, roads, trails
  • Blue — Water features
  • Green — Vegetation
  • Red — Highways and land grids

Two additional colors: Pink (built-up areas/civilization) and Purple (updated map information).

Map Symbols

Symbols represent natural and man-made features. They form a map language that is simple to read once you learn what they represent. Categories include vegetation, submerged areas and bogs, rivers/lakes/canals, man-made features, and highway/land grids.

Marginal Information

Before using any map, read the marginal information — it tells you critical details about the map:

  • Top left — Authority responsible for mapping (e.g., USGS)
  • Top right — Quadrangle name, state, county, area covered, map type
  • Bottom right — Road key, map name and state, map date, quadrangle location on state map
  • Bottom center — Scale ratio, distance bar scales, contour interval
  • Bottom left — Credit legend, magnetic declination diagram (True North ★, Magnetic North MN, Grid North GN), G-M angle
  • Around margins — Adjoining quadrangle names, road distances to nearest towns, latitude/longitude coordinates, UTM grid information

Map Scale

Map scale: one unit on the map equals 25,000 units on the ground

Map scale is the relationship between distance on the map and corresponding distance on the ground, expressed as a ratio (e.g., 1:24,000).

  • Large scale (1:75,000 and larger) — Used for tactical, administrative, and logistical planning. Standard: 1:50,000. Lots of detail.
  • Medium scale (1:75,000 to 1:1,000,000) — Used for operational planning. Standard: 1:250,000. Moderate detail.
  • Small scale (1:1,000,000 and smaller) — Used for general planning and strategic studies. Standard: 1:1,000,000. Very little detail.

Remember: the larger the number after 1:, the smaller the scale. Think of it as a fraction — 1/1,000,000 is a much smaller piece than 1/75,000.

Contour Lines

Contour lines showing elevation — points A, B, C, D at different positions

Contour lines are the most graphic technique for showing relief (elevation) information on a map. If you were to walk a contour line, you would never go uphill or downhill — you would eventually arrive back where you started.

  • Contour Interval — The vertical distance between each contour line, found at the bottom center of the map
  • Intermediate Contour — A standard brown line representing a line of equal elevation
  • Index Contour — A bolder/wider brown line with the elevation value marked on it

Contour lines close together indicate steep terrain; lines far apart indicate gentle slopes. Read between the lines — there could be rugged terrain, vertical bluffs, or deep ravines not shown at the contour interval.

Grid Coordinates

Grid square reference system

Subdividing a grid square for precision

Four-Digit Grid (1,000m accuracy)

To locate any coordinate: go RIGHT (easting) then UP (northing). Example: RP5904.

Six-Digit Grid (100m accuracy)

Subdivide the grid square into tenths. Example: RP598042.

Eight-Digit Grid (10m accuracy)

Subdivide further using a coordinate scale/protractor. Example: RP59850432.

Map Care

  • Fold maps to be small enough to carry but available without complete unfolding
  • Place in a waterproof packet to protect from water, mud, and tearing
  • If marking a map, use light lines that can be erased without smearing
  • If trimming margins, note marginal information (grid data, declination) on the back
  • All group members should know the map's location at all times

Terrain Features

Major Terrain Features

The 10 terrain features as shown by contour lines: Hill, Valley, Ridge, Saddle, Depression, Draw, Spur, Cliff, Cut, Fill

⛰ Hill

A hill is an area of high ground. From a hilltop, the ground slopes down in all directions. A map shows a hill by contour lines forming concentric circles. The inside of the smallest closed circle is the hilltop.

Hill: contour lines (left) and 3D terrain view (right)

⏳ Saddle

A saddle is a dip or low point between two areas of higher ground. A saddle is not necessarily the lower ground between two hilltops – it may be simply a dip or break along a level ridge crest. If you are in a saddle, you see high ground in two opposite directions and lower ground in the other two directions. Contour lines for a saddle normally resemble an hour glass.

Saddle: low point between two hilltops, shown with contour lines and hand analogy

🏔 Valley

A valley is a stretched-out groove in the land, usually formed by streams or rivers. A valley begins with high ground on three sides, and usually has a course of running water through it. If you are standing in a valley, three directions offer high ground, while the fourth direction offers low ground. Depending on the valley size and where you are standing, you may not see high ground in the third direction, but water flows from higher ground to lower ground. Contour lines forming a valley are either U-shaped or V-shaped. To determine the direction the water is flowing, look at the contour lines. The closed end of the contour line (U or V) always point upstream towards the high ground.

Valley: contour lines and 3D terrain view

〰 Ridge

A ridge is a sloping line of high ground. If you are standing on the centerline of a ridge, you will normally have low ground in three directions and high ground in one direction with varying degrees of slope. If you cross a ridge at right angles, you will climb steeply to the crest and then descend steeply to the base. When you move across the path of a ridge, depending on the location, you may find either an almost unnoticeable slope or a very obvious incline. Contour lines forming a ridge tend to be U-shaped or V-shaped. The closed end of the contour line points away from high ground.

Ridge: elongated high ground with slopes descending on both sides

⊙ Depression

A depression is a low point in the ground or a sinkhole. It could be an area of low ground surrounded by higher ground in all directions, or simply a hole in the ground. A map usually shows only depressions that are equal to or greater than the contour interval. Depressions are represented by closed contour lines that have tick marks pointing towards the low ground.

Depression: low point surrounded by higher ground, with tick marks on contour lines

Minor Terrain Features

∨ Draw

A draw is a less developed stream course than a valley. A draw offers no level ground and, therefore little or no maneuvering room while in its confines. If you are standing in a draw, the ground slopes upwards in three directions and downward in the other direction. The contour lines depicting a draw are U-shaped or V-shaped, pointing toward high ground.

Draw: less developed stream course with contour lines pointing uphill

∧ Spur

A spur is a short, continuous sloping line of higher ground, normally jutting out from the side of a ridge. A spur is often formed by two rough parallel streams, which cut draws down the side of a ridge. The ground slopes down in three directions and up in one direction. Contour lines on a map depict a spur with the U or V pointing away from the high ground.

Spur: lateral ridge projecting outward from higher ground

▮ Cliff

A cliff is a vertical or near-vertical feature. It is an abrupt change of the land's elevation. When a slope is so steep that the contour lines converge into one "carrying" contour of contours, this last contour line has tick marks pointing toward low ground. Contour lines very close together or touching each other also indicate cliffs.

Cliff: abrupt vertical change in elevation

Supplemental Terrain Features

▽ Cut

A cut is a man-made feature that results from cutting through raised ground, usually to form a level bed for a road or railroad track. Cuts are shown on a map when they are at least ten feet high. They are drawn with a contour line along the cut line. This contour line extends the length of the cut and has tick marks from the cut line to the roadbed, if the map permits this level of detail.

△ Fill

A fill is a man-made feature that results from filling a low area, usually to form a level bed for a road or railroad track. Fills are shown on a map when they are at least ten feet high. They are drawn with a contour line along the fill line. This contour line extends the length of the filled area and has tick marks that point toward the lower ground. If the map scale permits, the length of the fill's tick marks are drawn to scale and extend from the base line of the fill symbol.

Terrain-Map Association

Three Types of North

Declination diagram: True North (TN), Grid North (GN), and Magnetic North (MN) with sample azimuths

  • True North — A line from any point on earth to the North Pole. Represented by a star (★). The side edges of the map are the only lines guaranteed to run true north-south.
  • Magnetic North (MN) — The direction the north-seeking needle of a compass points. Symbolized by a line ending with a half arrowhead.
  • Grid North (GN) — Established by the vertical grid lines on the map (UTM grid). Symbolized by the letters GN.

The G-M Angle is the angular difference between Grid North and Magnetic North. You cannot follow a Grid North bearing with a compass, nor plot a Magnetic North bearing with a protractor, without converting between the two.

G-M Angle Conversion

When using a map — use a protractor to measure GRID bearings.
When using the ground — use a compass to measure MAGNETIC bearings.

The declination diagram in the map margin shows the relationship. To convert:

  • Ground to Map: Measure a magnetic bearing with your compass, then apply the G-M angle to get the grid bearing for plotting on the map
  • Map to Ground: Measure a grid bearing with your protractor, then apply the G-M angle to get the magnetic bearing for your compass

Example: Compass reads 49° MN, G-M angle is 24° → subtract: 49° − 24° = 25° GN. Conversely, map reads 25° GN → add: 25° + 24° = 49° MN.

The Military Protractor

GTA 5-2-12 Coordinate Scale and Protractor — degrees, mils, and multiple map scales

The GTA 5-2-12 is the most commonly used protractor for map reading. It has scales in both degrees and mils with a baseline and index point. With a protractor, the map does NOT have to be oriented. Align the protractor with grid lines on the map and the center on your position to read azimuths.

Orienting the Map

Compass placed on the map for orientation

Technique 1 — By Terrain (True North)

  1. Identify several landmarks on the map and on the terrain
  2. Visually orient the map landmarks with the terrain landmarks
  3. The map is now oriented to True North

Technique 2 — By Compass (Magnetic North)

  1. Lay the compass on the MN line on the map
  2. Rotate the map and compass together until the compass reads 0° (compass and MN line are aligned)
  3. The map is now oriented to Magnetic North

Technique 3 — By Declination (True North)

  1. Find the magnetic declination value in the map margin
  2. Place compass edge on the north-south edge of the map with front facing top
  3. Rotate map and compass together until the north arrow offsets by the declination value

Technique 4 — By Known Position (Magnetic North)

  1. Select a terrain feature you can see and identify on the map
  2. Sight the azimuth to that feature with your compass
  3. Align the compass edge through the feature and your position on the map
  4. Rotate map and compass together until the azimuth aligns with the index line

Map Information Categories

A map is read for four basic kinds of information:

  • Direction — Expressed in degrees or mils; azimuths are measured clockwise from north
  • Distance — Determined by the bar scale and map scale ratio
  • Position — Found by relating terrain features to map features, or using grid coordinates
  • Identification — Recognizing features using the language of lines, symbols, and colors
