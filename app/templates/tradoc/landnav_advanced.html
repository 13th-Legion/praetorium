Planning to Navigate

The Group or Alone

Every time you enter a wilderness area — familiar or not, alone or in a group — prepare as though you were going in alone. Leaders have been known to fail, and the group's best intentions do not always keep a group together.

Group Navigation

  • Choose your group carefully — if people are stubborn and inflexible at home, they will be even more so in the wilderness
  • A group must elect a leader who assesses the capabilities of each individual before making plans. The leader must avoid the appearance of indecision
  • Four types of leadership: Designated Leadership, Group Leadership (Active Followership), Peer Leadership, and Self Leadership — all equally important
  • Individual strengths and weaknesses must be carefully and privately considered by the leader
  • Physical and mental needs — medications, eyewear, hearing aids, socializing, eliminating boredom

Solo Navigation

  • The more experience you have navigating with others, the better qualified you are to go alone
  • All experiences, good and bad, become more intense when alone
  • The reward for trekking solo is a heightened awareness of potential dangers and stricter adherence to safety
  • A solitary wanderer becomes more conscious about leaving a note behind

Equipment — The 12 Essentials

Without a doubt the single most important item in your gear is YOU — a clear, cool head atop a body in good physical and mental condition, an adventurous spirit, and good common sense.

  1. A PLAN — never travel without it
  2. Map
  3. Compass
  4. Pocket knife
  5. Whistle
  6. Personal first-aid kit
  7. Insect repellant / sunscreen
  8. Waterproof matches
  9. Emergency rain gear
  10. Flashlight
  11. Water
  12. Food

Safety

  • Leave copies of your plans, destination, and schedule with a family member or Ranger Station
  • In a group — pace the hike to the slowest member. Start as a group, hike as a group, end as a group
  • Each member should study the planned route and have their own copy of the map & route
  • Check your approximate location on the map every 30 minutes to an hour
  • Bad weather or unexpected hazards — turn back. Do NOT take any risk

The Rule of Three's

  • 3 Seconds — time to decide to escape or act on immediate danger (MIND)
  • 3 Minutes — average survival time without breathable air (AIR)
  • 3 Hours — time before hypothermia/hyperthermia without shelter (SHELTER)
  • 3 Days — time before dehydration kills without water (WATER)
  • 3 Weeks — time before starvation prevents daily tasks (FOOD)
  • 3 Months — time without meeting anyone before solid depression sets in (HOPE)

Group Responsibilities

In a group, divide and rotate responsibilities. It is possible for one person to hold more than one position.

Group of eight navigation formation with assigned roles

  1. #1 SCOUT (point) — selects the best path, avoids hazards, waits after obstacles for group to catch up
  2. #2 NAVIGATOR — ensures the scout/group stays on course with map and compass
  3. #3 LEADER — stops group for rest, keeps members within visual/talking distance, makes decisions
  4. #4 PACE COUNT — tracks distance traveled by pace count and time
  5. #5 ASSIST NAVIGATOR — backup compass/map check
  6. #6 ASSIST PACE COUNT — backup distance tracking
  7. #7 HEAD COUNT / EQUIPMENT — during rest periods, ensures head count, equipment inventory, and nourishment
  8. #8 REAR (Assist Leader) — watches for hazards from behind, relays halt messages

Primary and assistant positions must compare findings: Navigator-to-Navigator (on course) and Pacer-to-Pacer (travel distance).

Terrain Study

  • Review park brochures, internet photos, maps, and all other sources to get a visual of the landscape
  • Detailed map study — it will show treasures and obstacles along the way
  • Elevations tell you where you may encounter snow (high elevations) in early summer
  • Contour lines show steep or gentle slopes on a planned route
  • Seasonal considerations: Has that small stream turned into a raging river? Is the water hole dried up? Wildfire danger?
  • Timing: An easy stream crossing in the morning can become dangerous by afternoon

Route Selection

Study the terrain carefully before selecting your route

  • Two types: a route that fits the group/person, or a group/person that fits the route
  • Will it be a loop route (A to A) or a one-way route (A to B)?
  • Match routes to the group's experience level, physical condition, and goals
  • Use binoculars — they save a lot of legwork. Scan very slowly, looking hard into rather than at the terrain
  • Select routes that can be modified for unseen situations: bad weather, trail conditions, injuries

Staying on Course

Wilderness navigation is a discipline when traveling off the beaten path. Do NOT rely on your "sense" of direction — rely on your compass and all navigation techniques available.

Human Reference Point

  1. Navigator #1 stays stationary while Navigator #2 goes ahead to the limit of visibility (with binoculars and walkie-talkies, the distance can be further)
  2. Navigator #1, with compass in hand at desired azimuth, signals Navigator #2 to move left or right until aligned with the compass bearing
  3. Navigator #1 catches up and the process is repeated

This is a slow process but very effective when needed. Keep track of distance and direction of each zig & zag for a running assessment of your approximate location.

Making a Reference Point

As a lone navigator, when a human reference point isn't available:

  1. At your current position, pile up rocks or other debris — make it large enough to be seen from a distance
  2. Set your desired azimuth (e.g., 125°) and calculate your back azimuth (125° + 180° = 305°)
  3. Walk forward on 125°, occasionally looking back and checking the back azimuth (305°) to the reference point
  4. Move left or right to maintain the back azimuth, which keeps you on course and eliminates lateral drift

Baselines (Advanced)

Azimuths can serve as baselines that let you explore a "trailless" area from your campsite:

  1. Create a baseline with the first azimuth from your camp to a landmark (e.g., 58° to Peak 1)
  2. Take a second azimuth to another landmark (e.g., 340° to Peak 2) to fix the campsite's exact location on the baseline
  3. With these cross-azimuths written down, you're free to wander the area
  4. To return: sight on Peak 1 to find your azimuth — if it differs from 58°, you're off the baseline. Head toward the baseline until the azimuth reads 58°
  5. Then sight on Peak 2 — if the azimuth differs from 340°, you know which direction camp lies along the baseline

Route Leg Planning

Multi-leg route with azimuths and distances plotted between checkpoints

For travel to a destination you cannot see:

  1. On the map, mark your start and finish points
  2. Choose the best route and draw the route legs
  3. Using a protractor, determine route leg azimuths and estimate distances
  4. Locate features or landmarks on the map that help identify each leg junction on arrival
  5. Write them down and follow your bearings (baselines)

If you have MN lines drawn on the map, align the protractor to a MN line and get the MN azimuth directly — no conversion needed.

Thumbing the Map

Keep your thumb on the map at your current position as you move. This simple technique helps you maintain situational awareness and quickly reference your location without having to re-orient yourself each time you check the map.

Additional Navigation Skills

Estimating Daylight

Reaching your destination before dark is critical. If you don't know sunrise/sunset times, use the hand method:

  1. Hold both hands at arm's length, palms facing you, fingers horizontal
  2. Line up the bottom of the sun with the top of one hand's upper finger
  3. Stack hands down to the horizon
  4. Count the number of fingers to the horizon — each finger ≈ 15 minutes of daylight

Estimating Distance with Compass Degrees

This technique works when you do NOT have a map. A lensatic compass is ideal for its accuracy:

  1. Take a bearing to a distant landmark (e.g., 75°)
  2. Walk perpendicular (at a right angle) a measured distance (e.g., 100 meters)
  3. Take another bearing to the exact same spot (e.g., 80°)
  4. Calculate: d = Tan(90 − (A − B)) × Reference distance

Example: A=80°, B=75°, Ref=100m → Tan(85°)=11.43 → d = 11.43 × 100 = 1,143 meters

Drawing a Map Without One

Using a lensatic compass to create a field map from compass bearings

When you have no map, you can create one from your campsite:

  1. Take two bearings to each visible landmark (one from camp, one after walking a measured perpendicular distance)
  2. Calculate the distance to each landmark using the compass-degree method
  3. On paper, draw your campsite and Magnetic North at the top
  4. With a protractor, plot tick marks at each bearing angle
  5. Using the compass straight edge as a ruler, measure and mark each landmark at its calculated distance
  6. Add trees, trails, and other details — you now have a usable field map

Conserving Energy

Rest Breaks

  • Take about 5 minutes of rest per hour of hiking to minimize lactic acid buildup
  • If you stop for less than 5 minutes — no problem
  • If you stop for more than 5 minutes — you may feel muscle soreness; extend the stop to 25 minutes to allow lactic acid to clear
  • Re-hydrate at every break

Rhythmic Breathing

Move at a pace that allows comfortable breathing and conversation. Synchronize walking rhythm with breathing rhythm. As load increases (steep terrain, humidity, heavier pack), slow your pace to regain controlled breathing.

The Rest Step

The mountaineer's greatest ally on steep ascents — briefly transfer weight from muscles to skeleton with each step:

  1. Step forward with right leg, keeping weight on back (left) leg, "locking" it straight
  2. Pause for a split second on the locked leg — bones, not muscles, support your weight
  3. Swing left leg forward, lock and transfer weight to the right (now back) leg
  4. Repeat — you may feel like a robot but you'll feel much better at the top

Walking Sticks

Walking sticks provide balance, reduce knee stress, and have many trail uses

  • Help maintain balance crossing streams, traversing hillsides, and crossing scree
  • Reduce stress on back and legs, especially uphill; reduce shock on knees downhill
  • Other uses: tarp pole, prop for pack, pushing aside brush, self-defense against animals
  • Experiment with two ski poles, one pole, or a wooden staff — do what's comfortable

Preventing Blisters

Blisters demand immediate attention — don't ignore hot spots

  • Make sure boots fit properly; treat boots with oils
  • Wear non-cotton sock liners; keep feet dry
  • If you feel a hot spot, stop immediately
  • Pre-apply moleskin to known blister areas before starting
  • Always carry a blister kit, even on day hikes

Weather Prediction

The Moon

  • Visible dark part of crescent moon → 24-48 hours of good weather
  • Ring around the moon → rain coming; count stars inside the ring — each star ≈ 24 hours away
  • Red-faced moon → moisture ahead (dust pushed by a low-pressure front)

The Sun

  • Rainbow or white band around the sun → drastic weather change in 12-24 hours
  • Red sun at dusk/dawn → fair (dry) weather
  • "Red sky at night, hikers delight; red sky at morning, hikers take warning"

Atmospheric Pressure

  • Low pressure (rain): Strong smells, birds flying low, smoke hanging low
  • High pressure (fair): Less smell, birds flying high, smoke rising straight

Plants

  • Pine cones open in dry weather, close when damp
  • Flowers bloom in fair weather, close when rain approaches
  • "Dew on the grass, rain will never come to pass"
